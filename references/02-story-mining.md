# 02 — Story Mining: from bullets to a credible story portfolio

## Goal
From partial material, construct **Student Profile Diagnosis → Story Portfolio (6–8) → Chinese long-form drafts (300–500 Chinese characters each, where supported) → Fact-check questions**.

## Profile first
Identify 4–6 differentiated abilities: finance modeling, empirical reasoning, methodological precision, communication, prioritization, adaptability, initiative, teamwork, teaching/research independence. Diagnose current persona, plausible career directions, motivation, genuine competence gaps. When career goal absent, offer 2–3 labeled possible narrative directions, not an invented firm job or offer.

## Find the actual friction, not a theatrical conflict
Ten prompts: data inconsistency; method does not answer managerial question; missing evidence; classroom vs practice; stakeholder disagreement; time/resource constraints; communication bottleneck; shifting scope; unfamiliar tool/industry; mentor feedback. A strong story can turn on one mismatched variable definition or untestable assumption; no need for artificial crisis.

Use internal STAR as audit, but **never output the headings Situation/Task/Action/Result as the narrative**. Preferred:
1. Real context and assigned work.
2. Specific question/constraint.
3. Initial approach and why insufficient.
4. What applicant **personally changed**, naming a real object (table, model, segmentation, variable, document structure).
5. Concrete deliverable and its verified use.
6. A new repeatable method that appears in next work.

**Cognitive-method change > heroic ending.** Prefer `instead of interpreting the anomalous trend immediately, I checked reporting definitions and period coverage` over `this experience taught me attention to detail`.

## Seniority scale
An undergraduate intern can: clean inconsistent data, redesign a comparison, build an assumption tracker, ask targeted industry expert questions, identify a risk, improve one slide, coordinate a handoff. An undergraduate intern generally should not be portrayed as the sole architect of major strategy, deal approval, hiring policy, or large P&L outcomes absent extraordinary supporting evidence.

## Domain examples (illustrative, not claims)
- Finance: reported earnings → repeatability → valuation drivers → scenario/sensitivity rather than one precise point.
- Economics: policy announcement → incentive and eligibility transmission → heterogeneous outcomes → measurable test.
- Wealth management: clients’ risk profile vs liquidity schedule → decision-useful dossier, without claiming personal investment advice.
- Management: marketing/event team delay → handoff/dependency map → better sequencing, without claiming firmwide reorganization.
- Data/AI: sudden indicator jump → base/measurement/definition change → distinguish true behavior from artifact.
- Research: hypothesis vs observable implication; recognize competing channels before asserting identification.
- Accounting: recognizing transactions vs presentation categories; ask whether classifications change interpretation.
- Marketing: qualitative comments → transparent coding scheme → comparison; do not invent observed outcomes.

## Story cards
Each card: `title | source role & dates | hard skill | soft skill | friction | action evidence | output | demonstrated change | possible programme use | unresolved verification`. Title formula: **发生什么｜核心能力** (e.g., `让模型假设可验证｜Analytical Rigour`). Different stories should collectively cover one rigorous technical example; one problem-solving; one feedback/communication; one initiative; one teamwork/leadership; and one adaptability example where available.

## Narrative progression
Find movement across domains, not just chronology: coursework identifies a framework → internship reveals boundaries → later experience refines judgment → study programme institutionalizes the inquiry. Do not repeat one analytical skill in four stories. Select by programme and prompt, not employer prestige.

## Completion check
Can an interviewer ask “what exactly did you do on Tuesday?” and receive a grounded answer? Could the supervisor corroborate it? Does every technical term describe something the student actually used? What would a different interpretation of the result be? If unclear, retain as interview hypothesis.
